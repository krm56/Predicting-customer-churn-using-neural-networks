# **Abstract** 

**Objective:** To evaluate and identify the superior predictive model for predicating customer churn within the telecommunications sector, specifically looking at models performance and how viable it would be in a real-world scenario. 

**Methods:** This study used the Duke 2018 telecommunications dataset, which underwent necessary data cleaning and preprocessing techniques to address the missing values and noise. To optimize model performance, advanced sampling techniques and scaling techniques were used. Four different distinct models were evaluated these being Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost and an Artificial Neural Network (ANN). Model were evaluated using a multi-metric approach, including F1-Score, ROCAUC, and an metric to evaluate the models hit rate at the top 10%. 

**Results:** When focused on the industry-standard top decile (top 10%) operational capacity, XGBoost emerged as the superior model, identifying 563 actual churners with a precision of 55.14%, while simultaneously achieving the highest ROC-AUC of 0.6758. While the Random Forest model captured the highest raw number of churners with a global recall of 0.8810, it was very aggressive, flagging 5,439 false positives. Financially, when operational constraints are not considered, Random Forest is the most effective money retainer for lower cost retention strategies. However, if a telecommunications provider decides to use more expensive, higher quality retention strategies, XGBoost becomes the financially superior model due to its more balanced approach, it identifies actual churners while significantly reducing the costs associated with false positive errors. Finally, the ANN was vastly outclassed by XGBoost across all primary metrics, proving that deep learning architectures are less effective than gradient-boosted trees for tabular data. 

**Conclusions:** This study concludes that XGBoost provides the optimal balance between predictive power, financial gain, and operational utility for telecommunications companies. This performance differs significantly from models like the ANN as the ANN is a black-box architecture, its practical use case decreases further. Beyond being the inferior model in this study, the ANN lacks the capability to inform the company of the most important features driving customer churn. Unlike the tree-based models, this lack of transparency hinders the creation of effective, data-driven retention strategies. Under standard telecommunications operational constraints, the "Top 10% Churn Capture" metric demonstrates that XGBoost is the most viable model for maximizing outreach to the most likely churners while simultaneously minimizing marketing waste. 

**Keywords:** Customer Churn, XGBoost, Artificial Neural Networks, Random Forest, Logistic Regression.

# **1 Introduction** 

Customer churn remains one of the most financially damaging challenges facing the telecommunications industry, while simultaneously being one of the most elusive and challenging problems to combat. As the telecommunications industry continues to grow at a rapid pace, so does the ease with which customers are able to leave their current provider for a competitor. Telecommunications companies now face a continuous and ever-increasing amount of pressure to retain existing customers rather than focus solely on acquiring new ones. Retention is a significantly more cost-effective strategy for companies than acquisition, as even a small reduction in customer churn can result in the preservation of millions in annual revenue. Consequently, the telecommunications industry has made predictive analytics a strategic priority in an attempt to identify customers most likely to leave and intervene before churn occurs. Machine learning models are now widely used and have become the industry standard for supporting companies in identifying potential churners; yet there remains limited clarity on which modelling approach offers the best balance between predictive accuracy, financial efficiency, and operational practicality. This dissertation evaluates four widely used churn prediction models—Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN)—using The Pacse (2019) dataset (originally derived from the Duke University cell2cell study). This study is guided by three distinct research questions that reflect the use of these models in real-world business scenarios: 

1. Which model provides the best financial trade-off between money saved from retained churners and money wasted on false positives? 

2. Does a deep learning model outperform industry-standard tree-based models when predicting churn on tabular telecommunications data? 

3. Which model performs best under the operational constraints typical of telecom companies, where only a small percentage of high-risk customers can be targeted each month? 

To address the third question, this dissertation introduces a specific evaluation framework: Top-10% Churn Capture, the Recall at the top 10%, and the Precision at the top 10%. These metrics measure how

many churners each model identifies within the highest-risk decile (the 10% of customers most likely to churn). The outcomes of this study provide practical guidance to telecommunications companies seeking to maximise customer retention while minimising resource expenditure. Furthermore, the findings contribute academically by comparing deep learning and tree-based models on tabular data, evaluating financial implications, and assessing suitability for top-decile targeting—areas that remain under-explored in existing research

# **2 Literature Review** 

Customer churn prediction has been studied for decades, but the way researchers approach the problem varies massively depending on the modelling techniques they rely on, the assumptions they make about customer behaviour, and the specific challenges they believe matter most in a telecommunications environment. As the industry has grown more competitive and customer expectations have shifted, churn has become one of the most critical problems for companies to solve. This has pushed researchers to explore a wide range of machine learning approaches. Earlier work in churn prediction tended to rely on simpler, more interpretable models like Logistic Regression; this was mainly because telecommunications companies wanted something explainable for executives rather than a model that was purely accurate. Over time, as datasets became larger and customer behaviour more complex, researchers moved towards more advanced models such as Random Forest, XGBoost, and eventually deep learning architectures. Even with this shift, there is still no clear agreement on which model works best in a real-world environment, especially considering the practical limitations companies face, such as only being able to target a small percentage of customers each month. This gap between academic performance and real-world usefulness is a primary reason churn prediction remains an active research area. 

A large proportion of literature continues to treat Logistic Regression as the baseline model. This is for good reason, as it is simple, fast, and easy to interpret, making it appealing for companies that need transparency in their decision-making. Many telecommunications companies still rely on Logistic Regression because it allows them to identify which features contribute most to churn—something that more complex models may struggle to show. (Wu, Ong, Yau, & Chong, 2021) demonstrate that Logistic Regression performs reliably when proper regularisation is applied, particularly L2 regularisation, which helps stabilise the model when dealing with noisy or highly correlated features. Their work highlights Logistic Regression’s sensitivity to multicollinearity, which requires careful feature engineering to avoid biased results. Another recurring issue in the literature is the model’s weakness regarding class imbalances. Telecommunications churn datasets almost always contain significantly fewer churners than non-churners, and this imbalance can severely distort the model’s predictions. (Nalatissifa & Pardede, 2021) demonstrate that applying SMOTE significantly improves Logistic Regression’s performance, increasing AUC from 0.52 to 0.597, which shows just how dependent the model is on balancing techniques. (Saha, Roy, Dey, & Ghosh, 2024) also emphasise the importance of feature scaling, noting that Logistic Regression relies on gradient-based optimisation and therefore benefits from standardisation. Without scaling, the model can behave unpredictably or converge poorly. 

As research progressed, Random Forest became one of the most popular alternatives to Logistic Regression, mainly because it offered a way to model nonlinear interactions without requiring extensive feature engineering. Random Forest works by building a large number of decision trees and combining their predictions, making it far more robust to noise and irrelevant features compared to models like Logistic Regression. (Chen & Guestrin, 2016) highlight that tree-based models, including Random Forest, 

9 

are inherently invariant to feature scaling, which simplifies preprocessing and makes them well-suited to telecom datasets containing a mixture of numerical and categorical variables. (Ahmad, Jafar, & Aljoumaa, 2019) show that Random Forest performs strongly on churn tasks, particularly when using around 200 estimators, and that under sampling can slightly outperform oversampling for this model. Their findings suggest that Random Forest is less sensitive to class imbalance than Logistic Regression, although performance can still be improved through sampling. More recent work by (Fatima, et al., 2024) introduces a mixed-data oversampling method that enhances Random Forest performance by optimising leaf sizes and feature subsets, demonstrating that even robust ensemble models benefit from advanced sampling strategies. Despite its strengths, Random Forest is often criticised for its lack of interpretability compared to Logistic Regression, which can make it challenging for business stakeholders to understand the underlying drivers of churn. 

As machine learning research continues to evolve, XGBoost has quickly become one of the most influential and widely used models for churn prediction. This is primarily due to its ability to capture complex interactions and subtle behavioural patterns that simpler models tend to miss. Unlike Random Forest, which builds trees independently, XGBoost builds trees sequentially, with each new tree attempting to correct the errors of the previous ones. This boosting approach allows the model to focus more on difficult-to-classify customers, which is particularly important in churn prediction where the minority class (the churners) is the group companies care about most. (Nalatissifa & Pardede, 2021) show that using focal loss with XGBoost significantly improves performance on imbalanced datasets, as focal loss reduces the influence of easy-to-classify non-churners and forces the model to prioritize harder churn cases. Their findings highlight one of XGBoost’s greatest strengths: its flexibility in handling imbalance without relying solely on external sampling techniques. 

Another advantage of XGBoost, shared with other tree-based models, is its ability to perform well without feature scaling. (Chen & Guestrin, 2016) emphasise that XGBoost’s tree-based structure allows it to naturally handle different feature types, making it ideal for telecommunications datasets that contain a mix of numerical, categorical, and derived behavioural features. This is a major benefit compared to models like Logistic Regression, which require careful preprocessing to avoid instability and bias. (Sikri, Jameel, Idrees, & Kaur, 2024) demonstrate that XGBoost performs optimally when the dataset is oversampled to a ratio of approximately 75:25, striking a balance between recall and precision. Oversampling too aggressively can distort the underlying distribution and increase variance, while undersampling can remove valuable information and increase bias; therefore, this middle ground appears most effective. Despite its performance, XGBoost is sometimes criticised for being harder to interpret than simpler models, although techniques like SHAP values have helped bridge this gap by illustrating how individual features contribute to specific predictions. 

While tree-based models have dominated recent churn prediction literature, deep learning has gained significant traction as researchers explore whether neural networks can outperform traditional models on structured data. Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) have achieved state-of-the-art results in fields involving unstructured data, such as images and text. However, their effectiveness on structured tabular data remains a matter of debate. (Marcus, 2018) argues that deep learning models are prone to overfitting, 

10 

require large datasets, and depend heavily on regularisation techniques such as dropout and early stopping. These challenges make ANNs less straightforward to apply to churn prediction, where datasets are often smaller than those used in computer vision. (Shwartz-Ziv & Armon, 2022) provide strong empirical evidence that deep learning often underperforms tree-based models on tabular data, citing instability, sensitivity to hyperparameters, and difficulty capturing feature interactions without extensive tuning. 

Despite these limitations, some studies show promising results when combining deep learning with advanced regularisation or very large datasets. However, most publicly available telecom churn datasets, including the one used in this dissertation, are static and tabular, which limits the potential benefits of deep learning. Consequently, many researchers argue that tree-based models like XGBoost remain the most practical choice for typical telecommunications environments. Furthermore, a major theme emerging from the literature is the disconnect between academic evaluation metrics and real-world operational constraints. Most studies evaluate models using accuracy, F1-Score, or ROC-AUC, which are useful in controlled environments but do not necessarily reflect practice. (Deloitte, 2023) highlights that telecom operators typically only have the capacity to target the top 5% to 10% of predicted churners each month due to limited resources. This means that ranking performance—how well a model identifies the highest-risk customers—is far more important than overall accuracy. Despite this, few academic studies evaluate models using top-decile metrics such as Recall at the top 10% or Precision at the top 10%, creating a significant gap between research and application. 

What becomes clear when reading through and  looking across all of the research is that churn prediction has been approached from several different angles, yet most studies still fall into the same pattern of evaluating models in a way that does not fully reflect how telecommunications companies actually need them. The majority of papers focus heavily on predictive performance without considering the practical realities of deploying these models in a real business environment. For example, a model might achieve a high ROC ‑ AUC score, but if it fails or has a lacking performance in correctly identifying churners within the top 10% of predictions, it becomes far less useful for a company that can only target a limited number of customers each month. This disconnect between academic evaluation and operational reality is one of the biggest limitations in the existing literature. Most studies treat churn prediction as purely academical problems when in reality churn is a real world business problem that requires a strict balance between several factors like predictive accuracy financial efficiency and operational constraints This is why metrics like Recall at the top 10% and Precision at the top 10% are so important, yet widely ignored in academic research. 

Another issue that appears repeatedly in the literature is the lack of financial evaluation. While predictive performance is very important, telecommunications companies ultimately care about the financial impact of their retention strategies. A model that identifies many churners but simultaneously produces a large number of false positives can end up costing the company more money than it saves. Despite this, very few studies attempt to consider and quantify the financial trade ‑ offs involved in targeting customers based on model predictions. Most papers tend to only report accuracy ‑ based metrics without considering the cost of contacting customers or the potential savings from preventing churn. This tends to be a major gap

in the literature, as looking at the financial aspect  is essential for determining whether a churn prediction model is actually worth developing and deploying . 

There is also not great amount research comparing deep learning models with tree ‑ based models on telecom churn datasets. While deep learning has become extremely popular in multiply fields, its effectiveness on structured tabular data remains uncertain. Some studies would suggest that ANNs can outperform traditional models when the dataset is large and complex, while others argue that tree ‑ based models like XGBoost consistently perform better on tabular data regardless of conditions. (Shwartz-Ziv & Armon, 2022) provides strong evidence that deep learning often struggles with tabular datasets, citing issues such as instability, sensitivity to hyperparameters, and difficulty capturing feature interactions without extensive tuning. Their findings suggest that deep learning may not always be the best choice for churn prediction, especially when simpler models can achieve similar or better performance with less complexity. However, the lack of direct comparisons between ANNs and tree ‑ based models on telecom churn datasets makes it difficult to draw firm conclusions. Finally, the literature rarely addresses the operational constraints that telecommunications companies face when deploying churn prediction models. Most studies assume that companies can target all predicted churners, when in reality they can only focus on a small percentage of high ‑ risk customers each month. This means that the ranking performance of a model is far more important than its overall accuracy. A model that performs well on full ‑ dataset metrics may still perform sub optimally when only the top decile of customers is considered. Despite this, very few studies evaluate models using top ‑ decile metrics or consider how operational constraints affect model performance. This creates a significant gap between academic research and real ‑ world application, as models that perform well in theory may not perform well in practice. The lack of operationally relevant evaluation metrics is one of the biggest limitations in the existing literature and highlights the need for research that takes these constraints into account. Without this perspective, it is difficult to determine which models are genuinely useful for telecommunications companies and which ones simply perform well in controlled environment.

## **3.1 Data Preparation** 

The dataset used in this study is the popular Duke University telecom (cell2cell) dataset provided by (Pacse, 2019) on Kaggle. This is a publicly available dataset widely used in churn prediction research. It contains customer demographic information, service usage patterns, billing details, and a binary churn label. This dataset is particularly suitable as it reflects real-world telecommunications behaviour and includes a mixture of numerical, categorical, and behavioural features. The data was initially explored by gaining information on the dataset as a whole and checking for missing values and variable types. Early Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) revealed, as expected via graphical display, a significant class imbalance of non-churners to churners see Figure 1. 

Several data cleaning procedures were enacted to ensure high-end data quality. Low-variance or irrelevant columns, such as service area and customer ID, were removed as they served no predictive value. Redundant features were also dropped, such as not new cellphone, which provided the same information as new cellphone; this was done to minimise multicollinearity and decrease the dimensionality of the dataset. The EDA also uncovered several heavily skewed features see Figure 2, and a critical part 

13 

of the data preparation was dealing with these via data transformations see Figure 3. Variables such as monthly revenue and monthly minutes contained extreme outliers—too many to be considered rare or unrelated to the observations—so instead of outlier deletion, a logarithmic transformation was applied. Log1p was used to ensure mathematical safety even if features contained zero values. This transformation was extremely important for less robust and sensitive models like Logistic Regression and the ANN, which perform sub-optimally and can induce bias when using skewed data, but work optimally with normally distributed data. 

Initially, missing values were handled using a hybrid approach of both listwise deletion and simple imputation; however, this approach later proved to be too aggressive and not suitable for the data. In a later iterative cycle, the method for handling missing values was handled solely by a simple imputer using a mean strategy. Importantly, this was done after outliers were treated and after the hold-out method of splitting the data into a train-test split to prevent data leakage. Finally, categorical variables were processed and turned into numerical variables using both label and one-hot encoding. After identifying the binary categorical attributes and the non-binary ones, label and one-hot encoding were applied respectively. Label encoding was only applied to binary attributes to ensure no artificial bias was induced via the encoding, while one-hot encoding was used for non-binary data.

### **3.1.1 Model Building and Evaluation** 

The dataset was split into an 80/20 train–test ratio. Four models were developed: Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost (with focal loss), and an Artificial Neural Network (ANN). While the initial plan involved using the industry-standard GridSearchCV for all models, it became clear that an exhaustive grid search was too time-consuming and not optimal for capturing the minority class. The strategy was therefore shifted to a research- and theoretically-led approach. For example, the Random Forest classifier was configured with 200 estimators following the exact recommendations of (Ahmad, Jafar, & Aljoumaa, 2019), ensuring the model remained generalizable. 

Different resampling strategies were applied based on model architecture. Logistic Regression and the ANN used SMOTE-Tomek, while Random Forest utilized SMOTE-ENN (Edited Nearest Neighbours) to better handle noisy minority samples. This distinction was necessary because linear and tree-based models respond very differently to synthetic data overlap. XGBoost did not require scaling due to its treebased structure, and Focal Loss was incorporated to improve sensitivity to the minority class without needing external resampling. 

The evaluation phase extended beyond standard academic metrics. Usual measures such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC were recorded to show how the models performed in simply predicting churners; however, these do not fully reflect real-world constraints. Telecommunications companies typically only have the capacity to target the highest-risk customers. Consequently, this study 

16 

introduced Top-Decile Targeting metrics to evaluate model utility. These included Recall at the top 10% (the percentage of total churners identified within the top 10 percent of high-risk predictions), Precision at the top 10% (the accuracy of predictions within the top 10 percent), and Actual Churners Caught (the raw number of individuals identified in the top 10%). These metrics ensured that the evaluation focused not only on how accurate a model was in theory, but how useful it would be in a practice-oriented, resource-limited business like environment.

# **5 Conclusion** 

This project initially set out to evaluate a range of machine learning models, specifically Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs), for predicting customer churn in the telecommunications industry, with a greater focus on operational utility rather than pure academic performance. The objective was to determine which model would be most suitable for real-world telecommunications companies to consider implementing, particularly where these companies must operate under strict operational constraints such as limited resources. In such environments, companies must be able to target and prioritize high-risk customers for their retention strategies. This project successfully delivered on these objectives; four models—Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and an Artificial Neural Network—were implemented, trained, and evaluated using both traditional and targeted metrics to reach a final conclusion on the best machine learning model for real-world telecommunication deployment. Results were produced in a way that increases interpretability, with these findings clearly identifying an ideal model for the business case. This model was XGBoost, which provided the strongest balance between predictive performance, operational capabilities, and business relevance. 

Although this project was able to answer the questions it set out to address, this was not achieved easily or without several limitations. The results for this project highlight a lack of optimal performance for all models when evaluated against academic metrics; for instance, none of the models were able to surpass an F1-score of 0.5, whereas several other researchers in the field have achieved scores well above this threshold. One of the main reasons for this is likely the limited experience and knowledge present at the commencement of the project, resulting in a lack of advanced data mining techniques to better preprocess the data, as well as limited EDA techniques to further understand the data and identify areas for improvement. This was apparent as little to no feature engineering, feature selection, or dimensionality reduction was performed. For example, methods like mRMR (Minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance), which have recently come to the author's attention, could have been used to remove uninformative data and useful redundant features, leaving only the features of utmost importance for predicting customer churn. Similarly, the lack of feature aggregation—combining and transforming existing features—could have produced more significant predictive features, allowing the models to learn from better-quality inputs. These omissions likely hindered the models' ability to recognise deeper, more important patterns in the data. 

Another limitation relates to the model training strategy. Due to time constraints and limited experience, the project relied on a simple holdout validation method rather than much more robust approaches such as k-fold cross-validation. Cross-validation would have allowed the models to be tested on the full set of data, which is particularly important as the dataset was not excessively large; it would have been beneficial to train the models on all possible data and predictor variables. Cross-validation would have also provided a more reliable estimate of model performance while simultaneously reducing the risk of overfitting or underfitting. The initial decision to utilize a simpler approach due to limited knowledge may

have ultimately limited the potential performance of the models. Despite these limitations, the project was still able to produce meaningful results and answer the research questions effectively. 

The work also demonstrated several strengths. The models were successfully implemented, the operational Top-10% evaluation was carried out correctly, and the analysis provided clear insights into how each model behaves under real-world constraints. The project identified XGBoost as the most suitable model for deployment, offering the best balance between recall, precision, and ranking ability. This finding is valuable for telecommunications companies seeking to maximise retention and minimise churn-related losses. The operational framing of the results—focusing on how many churners each model captures in the top-decile list—introduces a scope that goes beyond typical academic evaluations. 

Other important limitations to note in the project involve the size and scale of the dataset used in comparison to the scale of real-world telecommunications datasets. The dataset used here, the Pacse (2019) Telecom Customer Churn dataset (also known as the Duke University 2018 cell2cell dataset), contains approximately 71,000 instances. This is a small fraction of the scale seen in real telecom operations, which often have millions of customers. A larger dataset would have allowed for more robust training, better generalisation, and a more realistic assessment of model performance. Additionally, this project did not include a fully automated data pipeline, which is a key component in real-world churn prediction systems. Despite these factors, the project provides a useful foundation for telecommunications businesses trying to understand which machine learning models are most effective for their use cases. The primary audience for this work includes telecommunications companies, data science teams, and business analysts who need to identify high-risk customers efficiently. The findings demonstrate that XGBoost is the most reliable model for operational deployment, particularly when retention resources are limited and must be allocated strategically.
